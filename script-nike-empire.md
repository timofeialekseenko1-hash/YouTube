## ACT 1: THE ORIGIN (0:25–3:30)
**[B-ROLL: University of Oregon track, 1950s aesthetic, runners training]**

> NARRATOR: Our story starts not with a businessman, but with a middle-distance runner named Phil Knight, who was never quite fast enough to go pro.
>
> Knight ran track at the University of Oregon under a coach named Bill Bowerman — a man obsessed, some said unhealthily so, with shaving fractions of a second off his runners' times by any means necessary. Including the shoes on their feet.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: BILL BOWERMAN — TRACK COACH, OBSESSIVE TINKERER]**
>
> American running shoes at the time were heavy, stiff, built for durability, not speed. German brands — Adidas and Puma — dominated the world market. If you were serious about running, you wore German.
>
> Knight didn't accept that as permanent. In 1962, for a business class assignment at Stanford, he wrote a paper with a simple thesis: cheap, high-quality Japanese-made shoes could do to the athletic footwear market what Japanese cameras had just done to Germany's.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: "JAPAN CAN DO TO GERMAN CAMERAS WHAT IT DID TO GERMAN CAMERAS" — PHIL KNIGHT'S STANFORD THESIS]**
>
> It was a school assignment. Nobody expected him to actually act on it.
>
> He did.
>
> On a round-the-world trip in 1962, Knight stopped in Kobe, Japan, walked into a shoe manufacturer called Onitsuka Tiger, and — with no business card, no company, nothing but nerve — told them he represented an American distributor called "Blue Ribbon Sports."
>
> Blue Ribbon Sports did not exist. Knight invented it on the spot.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: THE COMPANY DIDN'T EXIST YET. HE PITCHED IT ANYWAY.]**
>
> Somehow, it worked. Onitsuka agreed to send samples.

## ACT 2: THE STRUGGLE (3:30–8:15)
**[B-ROLL: Cardboard boxes, small garages/basements, handshake deals]**

> NARRATOR: In 1964, Knight and Bowerman each put in $500 — about $1,200 combined in today's money for the coach's half of the deal — and Blue Ribbon Sports became real. Three hundred pairs of Tiger running shoes arrived from Japan.
>
> There was no office. Knight worked as an accountant during the day and sold shoes out of his car at track meets on weekends, going runner to runner, coach to coach.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: NO OFFICE. NO STORE. JUST A CAR TRUNK.]**
>
> He wasn't just selling a product. He was selling a category that didn't exist yet in the American mind — the idea that a running shoe could be a piece of performance engineering, not just a canvas sneaker.
>
> For years, growth was brutally slow and cash was brutally tight. Knight kept his day job because Blue Ribbon Sports couldn't pay him a living wage. Every dollar of profit went straight back into more inventory. He was, by his own later account, perpetually on the edge of running out of money — begging banks for lines of credit, getting rejected, finding another bank, getting rejected again.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: REJECTED BY BANK AFTER BANK]**
>
> Then, in 1971, the relationship that built the company almost destroyed it.
>
> Knight discovered Onitsuka was quietly negotiating with another U.S. distributor behind his back — preparing to cut Blue Ribbon Sports out entirely and take the business Knight had spent seven years building from nothing.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: THEIR OWN SUPPLIER WAS ABOUT TO REPLACE THEM]**
>
> This was the moment that could have ended everything. Knight had no factory of his own. No manufacturing relationships outside Japan. No brand identity independent of Onitsuka's "Tiger" name. If Onitsuka walked, Blue Ribbon Sports was just a man with a car trunk and no shoes to put in it.
>
> Knight didn't wait to be fired as a distributor. He made the terrifying decision to walk away first — and build his own shoe, under his own name, before Onitsuka could finish cutting him loose.
>
> He needed a name. And a logo. Fast, and cheap.
>
> A graphic design student named Carolyn Davidson was paid $35 — roughly two hours of her freelance rate — to design a logo representing motion and speed.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: THE SWOOSH — TOTAL COST: $35]**
>
> Knight's own reaction when he first saw it: "I don't love it, but it'll grow on me."
>
> The name came from a Blue Ribbon employee's dream about the Greek goddess of victory. Nike.

## THE TURNING POINT (8:15–10:30)
**[B-ROLL: A waffle iron, a kitchen, molten rubber — the classic Nike origin image]**

> NARRATOR: Here's the detail that sounds too strange to be true, but it's the hinge the entire company swung on.
>
> Bill Bowerman, still obsessed with shaving weight and improving grip, was staring at his wife's waffle iron one morning in 1971 and had an idea: what if a shoe's sole was pressed into a waffle-like grid instead of a traditional tread?
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: THE WAFFLE SOLE — INVENTED IN A KITCHEN]**
>
> He poured liquid urethane into the waffle iron. It ruined the iron. But the sole that came out was lighter and gripped the track better than anything on the market.
>
> The Nike Waffle Trainer, launched in 1974, became the best-selling training shoe in the United States. For the first time, Nike wasn't just competing with the German and Japanese giants on price — it had a genuine performance innovation nobody else had.
>
> This is the moment Nike stopped being a distributor with a new name, and became an actual shoe company with an actual edge.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: FROM DISTRIBUTOR → INNOVATOR]**

---

## ACT 3: THE SCALE (10:30–13:00)
**[B-ROLL: Fast montage — 70s/80s jogging boom, athlete endorsements, retail stores opening, stock ticker]**

> NARRATOR: What happened next was one of the fastest scale-ups in consumer product history.
>
> The late 1970s brought the American jogging boom, and Nike rode it perfectly — revenue exploded from around $10 million in 1976 to nearly $270 million by 1980, the year the company went public.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: $10M (1976) → $270M (1980)]**
>
> Then came the decision that turned a shoe company into a cultural institution: in 1984, Nike signed an unproven rookie out of North Carolina to an endorsement deal worth $500,000 a year — a huge bet on an athlete who hadn't yet played a single NBA game.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: MICHAEL JORDAN, 1984 — UNPROVEN ROOKIE]**
>
> The Air Jordan line sold $126 million worth of shoes in its first year alone — at a time analysts had projected $3 million.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: PROJECTED: $3M. ACTUAL: $126M.]**
>
> By the 1990s, "Just Do It" wasn't a slogan, it was a piece of the culture. Nike wasn't selling shoes anymore. It was selling identity, ambition, belief in yourself against the odds — the exact story of the man who founded it.

## WHERE THEY ARE NOW (13:00–14:15)
**[B-ROLL: Modern Nike World Headquarters, global stores, current athletes]**

> NARRATOR: Today, Nike does over $50 billion in annual revenue, employs more than 79,000 people, and its swoosh — designed for $35 by a college student who was paid in company stock she later sold for a few hundred dollars — is one of the most valuable logos on Earth.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: $35 LOGO → MOST VALUABLE SPORTSWEAR BRAND ON EARTH]**
>
> Phil Knight, the runner who was never quite fast enough, who invented a company on the spot to make a sale, who nearly lost everything when his own supplier tried to cut him out — is now worth over $40 billion.
>
> Remember that green Plymouth Valiant, and the trunk full of shoes nobody had heard of?
>
> It's still there — sitting in the lobby of Nike's world headquarters. They kept it. On purpose. As a reminder of exactly how small this all started.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: THE ORIGINAL CAR IS STILL ON DISPLAY AT NIKE HQ]**

## THE LESSON (14:15–15:15)
**[B-ROLL: Slower, reflective shots — sunrise over a track, close-up of running shoes]**

> NARRATOR: Three things stand out about how Nike actually got built — not the legend, the reality.
>
> One: Knight sold before he had a company. The pitch came before the paperwork. Belief came before proof.
>
> Two: the near-death moment — getting cut off by their own supplier — is what forced them to build something that was actually theirs. Nike only exists as *Nike* because Blue Ribbon Sports almost died.
>
> Three: the breakthrough came from obsession over a tiny detail most people would never notice — a waffle iron and a stubborn coach who cared about grams. Big empires are very often built on someone refusing to ignore a small problem everyone else accepted.
>
> **[TEXT ON SCREEN: SELL BEFORE YOU'RE READY. THE CRISIS FORCES THE REAL COMPANY. OBSESS OVER THE DETAIL NO ONE ELSE SEES.]**

## PRODUCTION NOTES
- **Pacing:** Keep Act 2 (the struggle) as the longest section — it's the retention engine in this format. Don't rush the near-betrayal by Onitsuka; let it breathe as the mid-video stakes spike.
- **On-screen text:** Every dollar figure and every quote should also appear as text on screen, not just spoken — viewers in this niche watch muted more than you'd expect.
- **Music:** Low tension/ambient under Acts 1–2, swelling orchestral under the Jordan deal reveal and the "Where they are now" section.
- **Thumbnail concept:** Split image — grainy photo-style shoe/trunk on one side, modern swoosh/stadium on the other, bold text "$1,200 → $150,000,000,000."
- **Fact-check before publishing:** Verify all figures (revenue milestones, Jordan deal terms, logo fee, Knight's current net worth) against current sourcing before recording narration — some numbers shift year to year and this script uses commonly cited historical figures.
